# Castle Survivor Review

**A dad builds Vampire Survivors in Three.js for his kids, and the result is more charming than it has any right to be.**

---

**Developer:** Cory Cowgill (solo, family project)
**Platform:** Browser (Three.js / WebGL)
**Genre:** Survivors-like (auto-attack horde survival)
**Played on:** Windows 11, Chrome, Xbox controller
**Time played:** Six full runs across all three knights and both maps at Ogre Level 0, plus one Ogre Level 1 attempt

---

## The First Hour

The title screen tells you everything about what Castle Survivor is before a single goblin appears. Three character portraits sit side by side: Dad, Knight Commander. Brennan, the Honor Bound. Parker, the Chivalrous. Behind them, a painting of a boy in armour stands shoulder to shoulder with his father while a tide of ogres crashes against castle walls. Below the portraits, a battlefield selector shows overhead map thumbnails and an Ogre Level ladder from 0 to 5, most of them locked behind golden padlocks.

This is a game a father made for his sons, and the developer wears that openly. The three playable characters are the family. Private Mode lets you toggle which knights appear on the select screen so the kids only see themselves and their dad. It is a small, deeply personal detail that no commercial studio would ever ship.

You pick Dad, load into Kingsfield, and the camera settles on a medieval village built from AI-generated 3D models. A cobblestone plaza sits below a curtain wall with iron-grated gates. Farms spread to the south: rows of pumpkins and corn, scarecrows, horses behind rail fences. A stream winds along the western edge with reeds and mud banks. Lanterns glow warm on the village streets. A blacksmith yard. A windmill behind the east farm. A fenced graveyard with a gnarled dead tree rising from its centre. It does not look like Diablo. It looks like a toy-box diorama of a medieval village, and that is a compliment.

Then the goblins come.

## Combat and Builds

Castle Survivor follows the Vampire Survivors template. Your knight auto-attacks in the direction you face. Enemies spawn from all edges and march toward you. Killing them drops XP gems that level you up, and each level offers a choice of three cards: new weapons, weapon upgrades, stat boosts, or relics. The run lasts 20 waves across roughly 17 minutes, ending with a showdown against the Ogre King.

The three knights play meaningfully differently. Dad wields a broadsword with a wide cleave and a Shield Wall passive that gives him bonus armour and HP. He is the sturdy generalist. Brennan carries a spear with a piercing cone thrust and starts with less health, making his early game genuinely tense. Parker swings a quickblade with fast attacks and a built-in dash strike, plus a modest armour bonus. Each primary weapon has its own evolution: Excalibur, Dragonlance, Tempest Edge. These are not reskins.

Beyond the primaries, there are nine weapons in total. Daggers fire automatically at the nearest enemy. Arrow Volley rains projectiles in a cone. Warding Shields orbit your knight and deflect arrows. Storm Call drops lightning from the sky. Trail of Embers leaves burning ground in your wake. Holy Aura pulses a solar-flare ring around you. Arcane Bomb summons a pentagram that detonates in a rolling orange fireball with debris and a smoke column. Several weapons are locked behind achievements: reach wave 5 for Arrow Volley, kill a boss for Arcane Bomb. You carry a maximum of four weapons including your primary, so every run forces a build decision.

The relic layer adds a second axis. Nine conditional passives, three slots. Phoenix Feather grants one revive. Hunter's Mark executes low-HP enemies. Wolfsbane Charm scales your damage with the crowd size. Berserker's Oath rewards fighting at low health. Warding Runes grants a brief shield after taking a hit. Most relics are gated behind run milestones, so your second and fifth runs feel different from your first.

On top of all this, a Forge meta-upgrade system persists across runs, with weapon mastery tracked by lifetime kills per weapon across five ranks. There is a Hall of Fame, feats, and the Ogre Level ladder as a long-term difficulty ratchet. The discovery drip is thinner than Vampire Survivors or DRG: Survivor, both of which gate dozens of items behind character unlocks and elaborate conditions. Castle Survivor has a smaller pool, but it uses the pool it has.

## The Horde and the Bosses

The enemy roster is compact but well-differentiated. Goblins rush you in packs. Rats are fragile cannon fodder. Wolves arrive in formation from wave 3 onward. Bats circle in rings. Ogres are slow, tanky bruisers that resist knockback. Bomb goblins sprint toward you and explode. The Goblin Shaman is the roster's standout: a purple caster that hangs back and heals every enemy within range every four seconds, with a violet rune telegraph on the ground. When a coven of two shamans arrives with a wolf escort at wave 14, you learn very quickly what target prioritisation means.

Goblin Archers are the single most dangerous regular enemy in the game. They keep their distance, strafe, and fire arrows every three seconds. In five of my six Ogre Level 0 runs, archer arrows were the top source of damage taken. In Dad's winning Kingsfield run, archers dealt 216 damage over 20 waves. In Parker's win, archers dealt 700. An off-screen archer warning system flashes directional arrows at the screen edge, and archers carry yellow ground rings to mark them in the crowd, but the sheer volume of arrow damage defines the mid-game experience. Learning to prioritise archers is the game's implicit tutorial.

Boss fights have real structure. A standard boss appears every five waves with telegraphed attacks: a charge down a red lane, a ground slam with a spreading ring, a summon phase. The Dragon Ogre at wave 15 volleys boulders across the arena. The Ogre King at wave 20 is a genuine final encounter. He has five times base HP, advances at full speed instead of lingering at throwing range, and enters guard phases at 66% and 33% health where he becomes immune for eight seconds while summoning an ogre wall, a shaman coven, and a wolf pack. A strong build with evolved weapons can finish him in about 25 seconds. A weaker build gets pushed and punished by the fury ramp that increases his speed and damage each minute.

In my Dad run on Kingsfield, I won at 17:17 with an evolved sword, evolved daggers, Holy Aura at level 5, and Storm Call at level 5, carrying Wolfsbane, Bloodletter, and Berserker relics. I finished with 6,904 kills and never dropped below 122 HP after wave 7. Brennan on the same map died at wave 6 with a level-5 spear and barely any secondary weapons. The early-game RNG can be brutal when your first few level-ups offer only stat cards and no weapon upgrades. Parker cleared Kingsfield at 16:47 with an evolved quickblade and orbital bombs, a comfortable win once his speed and dash carried him past the archer-heavy middle waves.

Darkwood was a different story. All three knights died. Dad fell at wave 8. Brennan at wave 7. Parker lasted longest, reaching wave 11 with 1,381 kills before the combined pressure of archers and bosses overwhelmed him. Darkwood doubles the wolf spawn rate and nearly doubles bats while cutting rats in half. The forest is dense with pine trees, and the cold blue moonlight makes threats harder to read at distance. It is a genuine step up in difficulty, not just a palette swap.

At Ogre Level 1, Dad reached wave 20 but died to the Ogre King after 18 minutes and 7,570 kills. Archers dealt a staggering 1,434 damage across the run, and the Dragon Ogre alone landed 563. The Ogre Level multipliers stack HP, damage, and spawn rates, and the difference between Level 0 and Level 1 is noticeable on every wave from about wave 8 onward. Levels 2 through 5 remain locked and presumably brutal.

## The Two Maps and Presentation

Kingsfield is the easier and prettier of the two maps. The warm directional light gives the cobblestone roads and timber-frame houses a Warcraft III quality. Splat-painted terrain blends grass, dirt, cobble, and mud convincingly. A stream with scrolling water and reed tufts runs along the western border. Farms with pumpkin patches and cornfields fill the south. Ground decals scatter leaf litter, hoof prints, and bone fragments in appropriate places. Thousands of grass tufts sway in a vertex-shader breeze. Landmarks give you spatial anchors: the village fountain in the central square, the border keep with red-capped turrets near the ogre fortifications, the watchtower in the north-west woods.

Darkwood trades warmth for atmosphere. The blue moonlight and deeper shadows transform the mood. Lantern posts cast warm pools along a forest track. A logging village clusters around the road with cottages, a lumber yard, and a market stall. A ford crosses a winding stream. The graveyard here sits under darker canopy with more ominous headstones. Light shafts pierce the clearings. It is legitimately moody.

The visual effects earned genuine surprise. Storm Call drops ribbon-geometry lightning bolts from the sky with forked ground arcs, scorch marks, and a blue screen flash. This is not a sprite. It is a multi-layered VFX sequence that would look at home in a mid-budget action RPG. Holy Aura erupts as a solar flare with a racing ring of fire, corona rays, and ember particles. Arcane Bomb deploys a pentagram sigil that detonates into an orange fireball that rolls upward, trailing burning debris on ballistic arcs with a dark smoke column. For a browser game built by one person, these effects punch far above their weight.

All art assets are AI-generated. Character models come from an image-to-3D pipeline. Environment props were created with Trellis and validated through an automated structural pass. Weapon and relic icons were rendered locally with Stable Diffusion. The quality is uneven. Some props, particularly the landmark buildings like the windmill and the border keep, hold up well. Others, like certain fence sections and tree stumps, have the slightly melted look that current mesh-generation models produce. The ground textures tile visibly at close zoom. None of this hurts the game at the standard play distance, where the warm lighting and grass tufts do most of the heavy lifting.

## The Meta Loop

Between runs, the Forge lets you spend gold on persistent upgrades. Weapon mastery tracks your kills per weapon across five lifetime ranks with a small damage bonus at each tier. The Hall of Fame records your victories, maps, and feats. Feats are milestone achievements that unlock new weapons and relics for future runs. The Ogre Level ladder provides the long-term difficulty challenge, gating higher levels behind victories at the previous tier.

This is thinner than the competition. Vampire Survivors has dozens of characters, each unlocking items, stages, and arcana. DRG: Survivor has a full talent tree per class plus a prestige system. Castle Survivor has nine weapons, nine relics, a handful of Forge upgrades, and six Ogre Levels. What it has works, but a dedicated player will see most of the content within ten runs and most of the unlocks within twenty. The Ogre Level ladder is the real endgame, and the jump from Level 0 to Level 1 already felt punishing enough that I wanted to come back with a better build.

## Performance and Polish

The game runs in any modern browser with WebGL support. Controller navigation is thorough: character select, Ogre Level, Forge, pause, game over, settings, and the level-up card picker all respond to a gamepad. Keyboard controls include WASD, number keys for card picks, and R to reroll. A settings screen offers master, SFX, and music volume sliders, plus toggles for screen shake, damage numbers, and hit freeze.

Audio is the game's single weakest pillar. There is one looping battle music track and a full set of synthesised sound effects generated through Web Audio. Sword swings sound like filtered white noise because that is literally what they are. Level-up chimes, coin pickups, enemy death pops, and boss roars are all procedural tone patches. They are functional but instantly identifiable as placeholder. When the visuals have been pushed this far, the gap between what you see and what you hear becomes the game's most obvious limitation.

Performance on a mid-range desktop with an RTX 4060 is excellent. The game holds a sub-1ms render frame even at wave 14 with 80 enemies, 7,600 grass tufts, and every VFX system firing. The enemy cap of 180 is well within budget. A GPU texture leak was found and fixed during development, and object memory is flat across runs. The HUD is clean and consolidated: health, XP bar, and character passive in the top left; wave counter, kill count, and timer in the top right; combo and streak counters in gold pills at top centre; boss health bar; weapon slots and a minimap at the bottom.

## Verdict

Castle Survivor is not trying to compete with Vampire Survivors on content volume or DRG: Survivor on progression depth. It is a family project built with generated art, synthesised audio, and a lot of craft in the places that matter: the weapon feel, the build decisions, the enemy design, and the visual effects. The fact that your three character choices are Dad and his two sons is not a gimmick. It is the reason the game exists.

The Kingsfield map at Ogre Level 0 is a polished, satisfying 17-minute run that nails the survivors-genre loop of growing from vulnerable to powerful. Darkwood is a genuine difficulty spike that forces you to respect the horde. Boss encounters have structure and threat. The lightning, the solar-flare aura, and the dynamite bombs are effects I would not have expected from a solo browser project.

Where it falls short: the procedural audio is impossible to overlook once you notice it, Brennan's early game is punishingly RNG-dependent, the discovery drip runs dry faster than the genre's best, and Darkwood's moonlit atmosphere sometimes crosses the line from moody to unreadable. The generated art has a consistency problem that the warm lighting mostly papers over.

But when I watch Dad cleave through a wolf pack at wave 12 while Storm Call cracks lightning from the sky and an orbital bomb detonates a shaman coven in a rolling fireball, all of it running at 60fps in a browser tab, I cannot help but respect what was built here.

---

## Pros

- **Three genuinely distinct characters** with unique primaries, evolutions, and passives
- **Standout VFX** (ribbon lightning, solar-flare aura, dynamite bombs) that punch above the weight class
- **Smart enemy design**, especially goblin archers and shamans that force target prioritisation
- **Structured boss fights** with telegraphs, phases, and a real Ogre King finale
- **Two maps with distinct mood and difficulty**, not just palette swaps
- **Full controller and keyboard support** across every menu
- **Charming family-project identity** with Private Mode and personal character names
- **Runs well** in any modern browser at steady frame rates

## Cons

- **Procedural audio only**: no recorded sound effects, one music track
- **Thin discovery arc** compared to genre leaders; most content visible within ten runs
- **Brennan's early game** is too dependent on weapon-upgrade RNG at level-up
- **Darkwood readability** suffers in areas away from lanterns
- **Generated art inconsistency**: some props look slightly melted at close zoom
- **No mobile or touch support**

---

## Score: 7 / 10

**A heartfelt family project that delivers where it counts: tight combat, real build decisions, and visual effects that have no business being this good in a browser. The thin content pool and placeholder audio keep it a tier below the survivors genre's best, but the craft is genuine and the foundation is strong.**
